How to organise a fantasy world

Start with what your story touches. Record the people, places, history and rules that appear on the page, link them to each other, and add depth only when a scene needs it.

1. List what the story needs, not everything

A world can absorb endless detail, and most of it will never reach a reader. Begin with the cast, the handful of places where scenes happen, one or two systems that drive the plot (a magic system, a political structure), and the past events that cause the story's central conflict. Everything else can wait until a scene demands it.

2. Give every fact one home

The most common continuity error is the same fact living in two places with two versions. Decide where each kind of detail lives: people in character entries, places in location entries, history on the timeline, rules and customs in lore entries. When something changes, change it in one place.

3. Build places as a hierarchy

Organise geography from large to small: world, continent or region, realm, settlement, building. A hierarchy makes renaming easy and lets you answer questions like "who rules the region this village sits in?" without searching. Add a map once the hierarchy exists, so the picture follows the structure rather than replacing it.

4. Write the timeline before the lore

A dated list of events exposes contradictions faster than prose does. If a war ended 200 years ago and a character's grandfather fought in it, the arithmetic either works or it does not. If your world has its own calendar, define it early so dates are consistent.

5. Link instead of repeating

A character's birthplace should point to the location entry, not copy its description. Links keep detail in one place and make it easy to see everything connected to a person, place or event.

6. Keep lore out of the prose

Notes can hold the full history of a religion; the scene only needs what the point-of-view character would notice. Keeping the reference material separate from the manuscript protects the pacing while keeping the facts available when you need them.

7. Review before each draft

Before starting a new draft, skim your timeline, cast and locations for gaps: characters with no origin, places no one visits, events with no consequences. Gaps are either things to cut or opportunities.
