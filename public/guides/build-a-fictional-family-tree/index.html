How to build a fictional family tree

Start with one anchor character, work outward by generation, record relationship types explicitly, and check ages against your timeline.

1. Start from an anchor

Pick the character or house the story centres on and build outwards: parents, siblings, children, then grandparents. Do not try to draw a whole dynasty at once; add branches when the story touches them.

2. Work generation by generation

Keep each generation on one level and give each person a birth year. Dates matter more than names: they let you check that a mother was old enough, that cousins are plausibly the same age, and that an heir could have been born before the coronation.

3. Record the relationship type

Blood relatives, spouses, adoptive parents, step-parents, step-siblings and half-siblings all affect inheritance, loyalty and secrets. Record which type each link is, rather than drawing a plain line. Mark who is the heir and how the succession works in your world.

4. Plan for marriages and remarriages

Noble houses marry for alliance. Note which marriages are political, which ended in death or separation, and which produced children, because half-siblings and step-families are rich sources of conflict.

5. Add the secrets last

Hidden parentage, a disputed claim, an unacknowledged child: record these separately from public knowledge so you can track who in the story knows what.

6. Check it against the timeline

Lay the tree beside your timeline. Wars, plagues and coronations explain gaps and ages. A tree with no history behind it often hides contradictions.

7. Link people to full profiles

A name on a tree should lead to the character's full entry, so a tree is a map of connected people, not a drawing.
